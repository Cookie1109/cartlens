A B S T R A C T
Frequent weighted utility pattern (FWUP) mining represents a significant extension within the 
domain of frequent pattern mining that aims to identify frequently occurring and significant 
patterns in quantitative databases. In todayΓÇÖs world, streams of data are constantly being 
generated rapidly, requiring appropriate mining methods to swiftly discover useful information 
from them. Previous algorithms for mining FWUPs were conceived for databases characterized by 
a predetermined quantity of transactions and are not amenable to direct application on dynamic 
data streams. Therefore, this study introduces the problem of mining FWUPs over dynamic 
quantitative data streams based on the sliding-window model. We propose two algorithms, 
named FWUDS-CT and FWUDS-DWT, to address this problem efficiently by reusing knowledge 
from previous windows without reprocessing the entire data. Furthermore, we also develop 
methods to perform real-time data updates. Empirical results show that our proposed method 
achieves superior performance in execution time and scalability compared to prior approaches for 
mining FWUPs over data streams.
1. Introduction
The frequent pattern (FP) mining challenge was initially presented by Agrawal and colleagues [1], becoming an essential problem 
in data mining. The task of FP mining is to identify all patterns whose occurrence frequency meets or exceeds a user-specified threshold 
on a binary transaction database. However, FP mining has a limitation when applied to real applications, which is that it only focuses 
on the frequency and occurrence of patterns and ignores other important information. Consequently, the problem of pattern mining 
has expanded in various directions, such as high utility patterns (HUPs) [2,3], high average utility patterns (HAUPs) [4], sequential 
patterns [5],ΓÇª A recent research direction of interest is mining frequent weighted utility patterns (FWUPs). FWUP mining focuses on 
both the frequency and the significance of patterns in quantitative databases. Various approaches have been developed to address this

In recent years, the rapid advancement of information technology has led to a massive increase in the volume of data. Humans or 
machines often generate these data in the form of data streams, and they are collected using sales data, social networks, and system 
logs. Extracting useful information from data streams under time and memory constraints has become a need and challenge in data 
mining. Mining FWUP in dynamic data streams is crucial for large-scale data processing systems, where information is perpetually 
evolving and necessitates immediate analysis of both occurrence frequency and the value contribution of data elements. Within the e- 
commerce sector, FWUP facilitates the identification of product combinations that are frequently purchased together and yield sub┬¡
stantial profits, thereby aiding in the development of dynamic recommender systems, enhancing personalized promotional strategies, 
and enabling real-time inventory management. In the field of social media analysis, FWUP contributes to the recognition of topics, 
content, or hashtags that not only have a notable degree of popularity but also possess significant influence, thereby effectively 
meeting the aims of trend identification, brand health evaluation, and misinformation detection. Furthermore, in the financial and 
banking industry, FWUP plays a vital role in monitoring and identifying prevalent transaction patterns associated with high monetary 
value or elevated risk levels, thereby enhancing the efficacy of fraud detection and risk management systems. The ability to mine 
FWUP swiftly in real-time and adapt to fluctuations in data and weights is a challenge that traditional pattern mining methods struggle 
to address.
Applying traditional FWUP mining methods on data streams is not suitable due to the data's nature and limitations. It is also due to 
the inability to use past computational results to optimize the mining process. Furthermore, real-world applications generate extensive 
amounts of data quickly, requiring the mining process to be performed at high speed in a single database scan. The sliding window 
model is frequently employed for extracting patterns from continuous data streams [4,10,11]. This model includes a window with a 
fixed number of the most recent data panes. As a new pane is added, the oldest pane is removed. Therefore, the sliding window model 
always ensures that the newest data is mined since often recent data provides the most value. Furthermore, pattern mining based on 
this sliding window approach requires a smaller memory footprint compared to other methods because it does not have to process and 
store the entire database. Several algorithms have been proposed for discovering HUPs [10,12], erasable patterns (EPs) [13], and other 
relevant constructs within data streams employing the sliding window methodology. Yet, it is currently needed to apply this to FWUP 
mining.
With the above motivations, this paper introduces the problem of mining FWUPs over dynamic quantitative data streams using the 
sliding window model, where item weights may change. Our main goal is to design efficient, single-pass algorithms that can effectively 
manage and mine these patterns, accounting for time-varying item weights, without needing to rescan the entire data window.
To achieve this objective, the primary contributions of this work are outlined below: (1) This study provides a formal conceptu┬¡
alization and framework for mining FWUPs over dynamic quantitative data streams, grounded in the sliding window paradigm. (2) We 
propose two new data structures, CTset (Circular Tidset) and DSWUN-tree (Dynamic Streaming Weighted Utility Node tree), for 
efficient data storage and processing for data streams managed under the sliding window model. The distinctive feature of CTset lies in 
its circular tidset management mechanism, which enables the reuse of the transaction identifier domain while preserving the logical 
uniqueness of transactions within the sliding window. This design effectively prevents numerical overflow and reduces storage 
overhead. Meanwhile, the DSWUN-tree structure provides a compact representation of all information within the sliding window and 
supports dynamic updates as new data arrive, eliminating the need for database rescans. From this, we can leverage the superiority of 
the WUN-list data structure to speed up the mining process. (3) We develop two mining algorithms for FWUPs over data streams using 
the sliding window model, named FWUDS-CT (Frequent Weighted Utility pattern mining over Data Streams using Circular Tidset) and 
FWUDS-DWT (Frequent Weighted Utility pattern mining over Data Streams using DSWUN-tree), which utilize the data structures 
mentioned above. (4) We conduct experiments to compare these two methods' runtime and memory usage on various datasets. The 
empirical findings indicate that both algorithms ensure reliability and correctness, with the FWUDS-DWT algorithm working more 
efficiently than FWUDS-CT.
Here is how the paper is structured. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 formulates the problem of mining FWUPs using a 
sliding window model on dynamic quantitative data streams. The section also covers basic concepts and knowledge of mining FWUPs. 
Next, the two algorithms FWUDS-CT and FWUDS-DWT for mining FWUPs over data streams are presented and described in detail in 
sections 4 and 5. Empirical evaluations and findings are reported in Section 6, and section 7 provides the complexity analysis and in- 
depth discussion about the proposed methods. Finally, the conclusion and prospective research avenues are discussed in Section 8.
2. Related work
2.1. Mining patterns on quantitative databases
Quantitative databases frequently emerge in retail industry databases. In a quantitative database, each transaction records the 
quantity of products purchased, and each product has a varying weight. This weight could be the price, profit, or the importance of the 
product within the database. Two methodologies have been advanced to extract significant insights from quantitative databases. They 
are high utility pattern mining and frequent weighted utility pattern mining.
2.1.1. Mining high-utility patterns
High utility pattern mining involves identifying all patterns with utility values that meet or exceed a predetermined minimum 
utility threshold specified by the user. This approach addresses the limitation of traditional FP mining, which is ignoring items that

bring great benefits but have low occurrence frequency. In HUP mining, the main challenge arises because the search space is typically 
combinatorically large when the anti-monotonicity property does not work, as the utility values generally increase with pattern length. 
To solve this problem, Liu et al. [14] introduced the Two-Phase algorithm. It uses the principle of Transaction-weighted Utilization as 
an upper bound satisfying the downward closure property. The Two-Phase algorithm is fundamentally derived from the Apriori 
methodology, so it has the limitation of scanning the database many times and generating many candidates. Next, the UP-Growth [15]
and UP-Growth+ [16], inspired by FP-Growth, incorporate several pruning strategies to improve computational efficiency during 
mining. Later, Liu and Qu [17] proposed the HUI-Miner algorithm, which employs a novel utility list data structure to mine HUPs. HUI- 
Miner only needs a single database scan to gather and store necessary information in the utility list. This structure enables rapid 
calculation of utility values. The FHM algorithm [18] proposed by Fournier-Viger et al. uses Estimated Utility Co-occurrence Pruning 
to quickly remove unpromising itemsets, thereby reducing the number of join operations. Next, Zida et al. [19] developed the EFIM 
algorithm and introduced two upper bounds: local utility and sub-tree utility. EFIM uses database projection and transaction merging 
techniques to minimize scanning overhead and improve HUP mining efficiency. Recently, researchers proposed many new HUP 
mining algorithms focusing on optimizing the utility-list-based representations. The HMiner algorithm [20] uses the new compact 
utility list data structure and the virtual hyperlink data structure. The HUI-Miner* algorithm employs the utility-list* structure [21]. 
The FOTH algorithm utilizes the novel IndexSet structure [22]. These algorithms demonstrated their efficiency in mining time and 
memory resources when applied to both dense and sparse datasets. Furthermore, researchers have extended HUP mining to variety 
other data mining tasks, such as HAUP mining [4], high utility sequential pattern mining (HUSP) [23], and skyline pattern mining 
[24].
2.1.2. Mining FWUPs
In the retail industry, FP mining delivers information about frequently purchased products, while HUP mining provides insights 
into the most profitable products. However, in practice, companies also aim to identify products that both bring high value and sell 
well to bolster their business strategies. To tackle this challenge, Khan et al. introduced a novel research direction known as weighted 
utility association rules mining [25]. The proposed approach comprises two phases: the first phase finds FWUPs, and the second phase 
generates rules from these patterns. To accomplish this, the authors proposed two new metrics: transaction weighted utility (twu) along 
with weighted utility support (wus). These metrics integrate both frequency and significance and notably utilize the downward closure 
property, which simplifies the process of pruning the search space. Subsequently, Vo et al. [26] introduced the MWIT-tree structure, 
which employs a tidset structure and achieves the mining of FWUPs with just a single database scan. Next, Nguyen et al. [6] developed 
the MBiS structure to enhance tidset storage optimization and improve the efficiency of mining. The MBiS structure stores information 
of consecutive sub-segments in tidsets to optimize memory usage, and the authors also introduced an efficient method for constructing 
new MBiS structures. Following that, Bui et al. [7] presented the WUN-set structure and its corresponding WUN-Miner algorithm. More 
recently, Nguyen et al. [8] introduced the FWUP-SWUNL algorithm based on the SWUN-list structure, and proposed several theorems 
to accelerate the mining process. Both the WUN-set and SWUN-list originate from the WUN-tree, however, the SWUN-list provides 
superior data compression, thereby reducing both execution time and memory footprint. Experimental findings suggest that the 
FWUP-SWUNL algorithm is currently the most efficient algorithm for mining FWUPs. Additionally, Tran et al. [27] utilized FWUP for 
text clustering, achieving higher accuracy than methods based on frequent patterns. Meanwhile, Nguyen et al. [28] developed the 
MINE_FWUIS and FAST_MINE_FWUIS algorithms, designed for mining FWUPs in hierarchical quantitative databases that organize 
items in a hierarchical structure, indicating interrelated item categories through a generalization relationship.
The aforementioned studies on FWUP mining have concentrated on static quantitative databases, in which the weights of items are 
constant. However, in practical scenarios, the significance of each product can fluctuate due to various factors, including discount 
strategies, item liquidation, and shifts in consumer behavior. Consequently, it is necessary to adjust the weight of each product 
appropriately. Nguyen et al. [9] addressed this challenge by mining FWUPs from dynamic quantitative databases (dDQB), where item 
weights may vary over time. Such dQDB are commonly encountered in practical applications. The weight of each data item could 
signify profit, price, or other benefits of products, and these values can change with each transaction. For instance, an itemΓÇÖs price may 
alter depending on the quantity bought (such as receiving a discount for bulk purchases) or vary seasonally (like different pricing for 
cooling appliances in summer versus winter). This leads to the highly practical problem of mining FPs from dQDB with changing 
weights. The authors proposed two algorithms based on baseline structures: the dFWUT algorithm, which uses a tidset structure, and 
the dFWUNL algorithm, which utilizes the WUNList structure, an extension of the N-List structure [29]. Experimental evaluations on 
both sparse and dense datasets have demonstrated that the dFWUNL algorithm is more efficient than the dFWUT algorithm.
2.2. Mining patterns over data streams
Data streams are characterized by their potentially infinite length, massive and continuous flow, unbounded nature, temporal 
ordering, and fast-changing features. Traditional pattern mining methods are not feasible for data streams because storing and pro┬¡
cessing the entire data stream requires substantial storage and computational capacity. Numerous studies have been conducted on 
pattern mining in data streams, concentrating on three primary models: the landmark model [30,31], the damped window model 
[32,33] and the sliding window model [4,13,34]. Each model has its own advantages and disadvantages, and selecting a mining model 
depends on the inherent attributes of the data as well as the specific needs of the user. In the landmark model, data from a specific 
starting point, known as a landmark, to the current time is considered (Fig. 1(a)). This landmark can be fixed, changeable, or resettable 
throughout the mining process. A key characteristic of this model is that all transactions within this time frame are equally important, 
rendering it appropriate for contexts in which both new and old data hold equal significance. Unlike the landmark model, the damped

window model weights transactions according to their chronological order in the data stream (Fig. 1(b)). In this model, older 
transactions diminish in impact; therefore, their significance and weight decrease with each new data addition. Users define a decay 
factor, which ranges from 0 to 1, to dictate this decrease. Each time a new transaction is added, the weights of previous transactions are 
multiplicatively reduced by this decay factor over time. While the damped window model emphasizes the differences between 
transactions, it demands considerable resources to maintain and manage the entire dataset. The sliding window mechanism is another 
popular approach in data stream pattern mining. In this model, the data stream is segmented into equal-sized data panes. A window, 
which is fixed in size and contains multiple panes retains only the most contemporaneous data. As new data panes enter, the oldest 
pane is discarded to keep the window size constant (Fig. 1(c)). Pattern mining is then conducted on this refreshed window. The sliding 
window model facilitates quick extraction of useful information from the latest data, although selecting the optimal pane and window 
sizes poses challenges for users.
Pattern mining algorithms for data streams have been extensively researched and applied to various types, including FPs [35,36], 
HUPs [10,33,37], EPs [13,32], among others, achieving notable success. Generally, these algorithms focus on solving the following 
main problems. Firstly, the algorithms are designed to minimize data scanning to just one pass, given the large volume and rapid influx 
of data streams. Secondly, new data structures have been developed to efficiently store, manage, update, and discover patterns when 
new data arrives. Thirdly, the algorithms are crafted to ensure reliability, accuracy, and efficient use of resources. However, as of now, 
there is no existing research on mining FWUPs in data streams.
A critical analysis of prior literature, summarized in Table 1, reveals several significant research gaps that motivate our proposed 
approach. Most existing high-performance FWUP mining algorithms [7,8] are fundamentally designed for static databases. Their 
reliance on full data access and the assumption of fixed datasets render them unsuitable for the transient, high-velocity nature of data 
streams. Furthermore, even the most relevant state-of-the-art (SOTA) dynamic algorithm, dFWUNL [9], which is highly effective for 
quantitative databases, wherein the weights assigned to items may vary, suffers from a major inefficiency. It was not designed for the 
sliding window model and requires two complete scans of the current window. This repeated re-scanning is computationally pro┬¡
hibitive for real-time, single-pass stream processing. Concurrently, while the field of stream mining has produced numerous pattern 
mining algorithms, these methods do not address our specific problem. They are not designed to handle the combined challenges of 
FWUPs, quantitative databases, and dynamically varying item weights. Therefore, a clear research gap exists for an efficient frame┬¡
work that can reuse previously computed information, rapidly update data in real time as the window slides, and extract all FWUPs 
from dynamic quantitative data streams, precisely the challenge that our proposed methods aim to address.

4.1. Circular Tidset data structure
The first proposed algorithm for mining FWUPs over dynamic quantitative data streams is named FWUDS-CT, which utilizes the

denoted as CTset, which is an improvement of the tidset structure, specifically designed to accommodate data streams more efficiently.

4.2. Maintaining CTsets over data stream
After the initialization of the first window, the sliding window process commences, involving changes to the data within the 
window: transactions from the oldest pane are removed, and new ones are added. This requires updating the CTset for each item to 
reflect the data of the current window. When adding a new pane into the window, the lcTid for each new transaction is calculated 
according to Definition 5. Then, the lcTid is added to the end of the CTset of the items appearing in that transaction. Consequently, the 
order of the newest transaction information in the CTset will be at the end of the list, while the beginning of the list will contain the 
lcTid of the older transactions. The operation of removing the oldest pane from the window is more complex and is performed before 
the addition operation. We can determine the lcTids of the oldest pane as follows. 
Theorem 1.
Suppose the lcTid of the newest transaction in the current window has a value of n (the window is full), then the oldest pane in 
this window will contain transactions with lcTid value in [n +1, n +|P|] if n < |W|, and in [1, |P|] if n = |W|.
Proof: Clearly, with n = |W|, the lcTid value of the transactions in this window are sequentially {1,2,Γï»,n ΓêÆ1,n}, thus the oldest pane 
will include transactions with lcTid as [1,2,Γï»|P| ΓêÆ1,|P|}. Similarly, with n < |W|, the current window contains lcTids sequentially {n +
1,n + 2,Γï»,|W|,1,2,Γï»,n}, so oldest pane includes lcTids value {n + 1,n + 2,Γï»,n + |P|}.
Based on Theorem 1, we traverse the CTset of all items appearing in the window from the beginning to the end of the list. For each 
item, if the lcTids value belongs to the oldest pane, it will be removed from the CTset. Otherwise, we terminate the process for the 
current item and proceed to the next one, as the following transactions are certainly not from the oldest pane. Given the need to add 
and remove elements at both ends of the list, we use the linked list data structure to manage the CTset. The pseudocode for these two 
operations is shown in Fig. 6.

5. Mining FWUPs using DSWUN-tree data structure over data streams
5.1. The DSWUN-tree data structure
Nguyen et al. introduced the WUNList data structure [9] to discover FWUPs from dQDB, achieving superior experimental results in 
terms of both runtime and memory usage. The WUNList is generated from the WUN-tree structure, which is an extension of the N-list 
structure [29]. The WUN-tree compresses the database in tree nodes and eliminates infrequent items. However, the WUN-tree is 
limited to databases with a fixed number of transactions and requires up to two scans to be constructed. Building the WUN-tree is time- 
consuming, especially as the data size increases. Therefore, this structure is unsuitable to apply the sliding window model over data 
streams. To address this issue, we propose a new tree structure, named the DSWUN-tree, designed specifically to efficiently mine 
FWUPs within data streams. 
Definition 8.
The DSWUN-tree is a tree structure consisting of a root node and a set of child nodes. Each node in the DSWUN-tree 
includes six fields (name, weight, pre, post, children, and parent), where name represents the item corresponding to the node, weight is the 
total twu of transactions passing through the node, pre is the order of the node in preorder traversal, post is the order in post-order 
traversal, children is the list of child nodes, and parent is the parent of the current node.
Unlike the WUN-tree, the proposed DSWUN-tree structure additionally stores the parent node to facilitate tree updates when data

5.2. Updating the DSWUN-tree
In the sliding window phase, when a new pane is added, the oldest pane is removed from the DSWUN-tree because it is no longer in 
the current window. Utilizing the TAILLIST and the parent node information stored at each node, we can easily extract the transactions 
in the oldest pane and delete them from the DSWUN-tree. Fig. 12 describes the algorithm for updating the DSWUN-tree. First, each

transaction ti. Next, we reduce the weight of the current node by the twu value of this transaction. If the weight of this node diminishes 
to zero, it will be removed from the tree. Then, we move up to its parent node. This process repeats until we reach the root node of the 
DSWUN-tree (lines 4ΓÇô7). Finally, in line 8, we remove the first node from the TAILLIST and start the next iteration. At the end of the 
update process, we obtain the DSWUN-tree for the new window.

In this section, we present the empirical findings derived from the comparative analysis of two algorithms designed for mining 
FWUPs in dynamic quantitative data streams using the sliding window mechanism. The two proposed algorithms, FWUDS-CT and 
FWUDS-DWT, are compared with a modified version of the dFWUNL algorithm [9] (denoted as dFWUNL-stream). The dFWUNL al┬¡

efficiency of the proposed models; and (4) scalability, assessing the performance of each algorithm under increasing data volumes.

515,000P, 980,000P, and 4,000,000P, respectively. Notably, as the window size increases, the difference in the total time required for 
data construction and updates between the algorithms becomes more pronounced.
6.3. Runtime for FWUP mining
Fig. 20 shows the total mining time of FWUPs for the FWUDS-CT, FWUDS-DWT, and dFWUNL-stream algorithms on datasets with 
varying minwus thresholds. The mining time is measured from the start of the algorithm until it terminates. The window size and pane 
size are fixed, as described in Section 6.2. Due to the significant difference in runtime between the algorithms, we use a logarithmic 
scale (log2) for the runtime to facilitate comparison and presentation on the chart. The results show a trend where, as the minwus 
threshold decreases, the mining time of the algorithms increases. This is because, as the threshold lowers, the number of FWUPs in┬¡
creases, causing the algorithms to take more time to find all the satisfying patterns. Although the FWUDS-CT algorithm is efficient in 
data construction and updates, it takes more time to mine FWUPs. Moreover, with very small thresholds or very large datasets, such as 
Susy, the FWUDS-CT algorithm fails due to memory overflow. In contrast, the FWUDS-DWT algorithm demonstrates superiority, 
achieving the shortest runtime among the three algorithms tested. This is due to the efficiency of data updates using the DSWUN-tree 
structure, combined with the optimization of the mining process through the WUNList structure. For example, on the Retail dataset 
with a minwus threshold of 0.2%, the FWUDS-CT algorithm took 223 s, the dFWUNL-stream algorithm took 25 s, while the FWUDS- 
DWT algorithm took only 13.5 s. On the BMS-POS dataset with a threshold of 0.4%, the total mining times of the FWUDS-CT, FWUDS- 
DWT, and dFWUNL-stream algorithms after 50 times sliding windows were 845 s, 105.8 s, and 174.9 s, respectively.
6.4. Memory usage
To ensure a comprehensive evaluation, we conducted a systematic analysis of the peak memory consumption of the proposed 
algorithms compared with the baseline algorithm. This analysis is particularly crucial, as memory efficiency is often a primary 
constraint in data stream processing. The peak memory consumption (measured in megabytes, MB) was recorded for all algorithms 
across five datasets under varying minwus thresholds, as illustrated in Fig. 21. The window size and pane size parameters were 
configured as described in Subsection 6.2.
The experimental results highlight distinct differences and inherent design trade-offs among the algorithms. Overall, as the minwus 
threshold decreases, a greater number of FWUPs satisfy the condition, leading to increased memory usage across all methods. The 
FWUDS-DWT algorithm consistently exhibits the highest memory usage across all datasets. Specifically, its memory consumption 
remains nearly constant for certain datasets, approximately 1500 MB on Susy and 835 MB on Kosarak, regardless of the minwus value. 
This stability arises because the FWUDS-DWT algorithm maintains the entire DSWUN-tree structure in main memory, representing all 
transactions within the current sliding window. Furthermore, the DSWUN-tree structure is inherently complex and information- 
intensive, requiring substantial storage space. Although this design incurs considerable memory overhead, it enables FWUDS-DWT 
to achieve high runtime efficiency and support rapid data updates.
Conversely, the FWUDS-CT algorithm demonstrates superior memory efficiency on sparse datasets but performs less effectively on 
dense data. On the Retail and BMS-POS datasets, it is the most memory-efficient method, consuming only about 38.1 MB on Retail at 
minwus = 0.03, compared with 222 MB for FWUDS-DWT and 134.9 MB for dFWUNL-stream. This efficiency arises from its lightweight 
CTset data structure, which minimizes memory allocation in sparse data environments. However, when applied to dense datasets, the 
size of the item transaction identifier lists grows rapidly, resulting in significant memory expansion. Consequently, FWUDS-CT exhibits 
excessive runtime on Accidents at low thresholds and fails to execute on the large Susy dataset due to out-of-memory errors.
Meanwhile, the baseline algorithm, dFWUNL-stream, also demonstrates notable memory efficiency across datasets. This advantage 
stems from its strategy of constructing the WUN-tree only for items satisfying the minwus threshold and discarding it after generating 
the final WUN-list in each independent window. In contrast, both proposed algorithms retain all window data to support rapid updates. 
For instance, on the Retail dataset, FWUDS-CT consumes 33.4 MB, FWUDS-DWT consumes 110.4 MB, whereas dFWUNL-stream re┬¡
quires only 25.7 MB.

7. Complexity analysis and discussion
In this section, we elucidate the intricate complexity analysis pertaining to the methodologies that have been proposed. Based on 
the analytical results, we also discuss the inherent limitations of the algorithms under various real-world data stream scenarios. The 
time complexity of the FWUDS-CT algorithm consists of two primary components: window maintenance and mining. When a new pane 
of size p arrives, the window update process is executed using the InsertNewPane and RemoveOldestPane procedures (the latter is 
invoked when the window is full). The InsertNewPane procedure iterates through p transactions, each containing up to lmax items, 
where lmax denotes the maximum transaction length in the current window. Each item requires an lcTid insertion operation with a 
constant cost of O(1), leading to a time complexity of O(p ├ù lmax). For the RemoveOldestPane procedure, the algorithm iterates 
through m CTsets, where m represents the number of unique items in the window. The total number of lcTid checking and deletion 
operations across all m lists is bounded by O(p ├ù lmax), resulting in an overall cost of O(m + p ├ù lmax). Therefore, the total time 
complexity for window maintenance per new pane is O(m + p ├ù lmax). Regarding the mining cost, the process begins with a recursive 
call to MineFWUPs_CTsets. The core operation involves generating candidate patterns and computing their CTsets via the CTset┬¡
s_Intersection procedure. This intersection, performed linearly on two CTsets with a maximum size of ctmax, has a time complexity of 
O(ctmax). Let I1 denote the set of items satisfying the minwus threshold. In the worst case, the algorithm explores the entire search space, 
where the total number of candidate patterns is 2|I1|. Consequently, the overall time complexity of the mining phase is O(ctmax ├ù 2|I1|).
For the FWUDS-DWT algorithm, the initialization of the DSWUN-tree involves processing n = w ├ù p transactions in the first 
window, where w denotes the number of panes and p the pane size. For each transaction, all items are sorted and inserted into the tree. 
Thus, the one-time initialization cost of the tree is O(n ├ù lmax ├ù log(lmax) ). For each incoming pane, the total time cost consists of tree 
update and mining phases. The update phase, performed by the Update_DSWUN-tree procedure, includes two operations: inserting p 
new transactions and evicting p obsolete ones. Inserting p new transactions requires p calls to InsertTree, each costing 
O(lmax ├ù log(lmax) ), resulting in a total cost of O(p ├ù lmax ├ù log(lmax) ). The eviction phase also iterates p times, and each iteration 
traverses from a leaf node in the TAILLIST up to the root, incurring O(lmax) time per operation. Therefore, the total tree update time per 
pane is O(p ├ù lmax ├ù log(lmax) ). The mining phase begins with generating the WUNList for the items in I1, which takes O(|I1| ├ù wunlmax), 
where wunlmax represents the maximum WUNList length. Subsequently, the recursive procedure MineFWUPs_WUNList is invoked.

The FWUDS-DWT algorithm explores a set-enumeration tree to mine FWUPs. In the worst case, the search space is O(2|I1|), corre┬¡
sponding to 2|I1| nodes. At each node, an intersection of two WUNLists is performed to generate a new patternΓÇÖs WUNList, with a time 
complexity of O(wunlmax). Consequently, the overall time complexity for the mining process per window is O(wunlmax ├ù 2|I1|).
Based on the theoretical analysis, both proposed algorithms exhibit highly efficient data update performance during window 
sliding. The key reason lies in their pane-based incremental update mechanism, which eliminates the need to reprocess all n trans┬¡
actions in the sliding window, a requirement of the baseline dFWUNL-stream algorithm. Regarding mining time complexity, both 
algorithms theoretically possess exponential cost in the worst-case scenario. This is a common characteristic of pattern mining al┬¡
gorithms. However, in practice, the FWUDS-DWT algorithm demonstrates superior performance. This is attributed to FWUDS-DWT's 
use of the WUNList structure, which features data compression and progressively shortens after each intersection, thereby accelerating 
the pattern generation process compared to the CTset structure. In terms of space complexity, both algorithms must retain the complete 
data of the current window, requiring O(w ├ù p ├ù lmax) memory. However, the DSWUN-tree structure is structurally more complex than 
CTset and also includes a TAILLIST structure requiring O(n) memory. Consequently, the DSWUN-tree demands more memory in 
practice, particularly on sparse datasets. Furthermore, both algorithms employ a depth-first search strategy for FWUP mining. The 
corresponding space complexity for this phase is O(ctmax ├ù |I1|2) for FWUDS-CT and O(wunlmax ├ù |I1|2) for FWUDS-DWT. As a result, on 
large and dense datasets, where items appear in numerous transactions, the FWUDS-CT algorithm tends to consume significantly more 
memory during mining.
Thus, the FWUDS-DWT algorithm sacrifices additional memory in exchange for its compressed DSWUN-tree structure and concise 
WUNLists, enabling faster mining performance. In contrast, the FWUDS-CT algorithm emphasizes an extremely lightweight and simple 
CTset structure but incurs a higher computational cost due to longer CTsets. This trade-off directly determines their respective 
application scenarios, limitations, and scalability. Specifically, FWUDS-DWT is the optimal choice for systems with abundant memory 
resources and high-frequency mining requirements, as its data compression substantially reduces the cost of WUNList intersections, 
resulting in superior runtime efficiency. Conversely, the FWUDS-CT algorithm is more suitable for applications where the input data is 
predominantly sparse, benefiting from its compact structure and minimal memory footprint. In terms of limitations and scalability, the 
bottlenecks of the two algorithms differ fundamentally. For FWUDS-DWT, the core limitation is memory. The requirement to maintain 
the DSWUN-tree structure for the entire current window causes memory to grow, limiting scalability for very large windows on sparse 
datasets. Meanwhile, the limitation of FWUDS-CT lies in processing time, as its mining performance degrades substantially when ctmax 
increases or when the minwus threshold is set too low on dense datasets.
Furthermore, the proposed methods exhibit several additional limitations related to scalability and real-world edge cases. First, 
under extremely high-velocity data streams, the update phase remains highly efficient and capable of real-time operation. However, 
the mining phase is inherently time-consuming. When the data ingestion rate becomes excessively high, the system may face a sit┬¡
uation where updates occur in real time, but mining cannot keep pace. Consequently, the generated patterns may lag behind the most 
recent data, reducing the systemΓÇÖs responsiveness to rapid changes. This challenge with high-dimensionality is not unique to pattern 
mining but is a well-known problem across the broader field of data mining. Second, for high-dimensional data, characterized by an 
extremely large number of unique items, the FWUDS-CT algorithm is directly affected, as it must maintain CTsets for all items. As the 
number of dimensions increases, this results in considerable resource overhead for both storage and updates, thereby limiting its 
scalability in such environments. Third, the current framework does not incorporate an explicit mechanism for handling noisy data. In 
real-world data streams (e.g., those affected by sensor errors or transmission noise), this limitation may lead to spurious patterns or 
distort weighted utility calculations. While robust noise-filtering or noise-tolerant mining techniques were beyond the scope of this 
study, we acknowledge this as an important limitation and identify it as a promising avenue for future research. Finally, another edge 
case arises from rapidly fluctuating weighted utility. The DSWUN-tree achieves high compression by ordering items according to their 
wus-order. However, frequent or abrupt changes in these weights can disrupt this ordering. As a result, the updated DSWUN-tree may 
exhibit lower compression efficiency, leading to an increase in both tree size and WUNList length, thereby degrading overall 
performance.
8. Conclusion
This paper introduces the problem of mining FWUPs from dynamic quantitative data streams using a sliding window model, where 
item weights can be adjusted. We propose two algorithms, FWUDS-CT and FWUDS-DWT, specifically designed to manage the dynamic 
changes in item weights and the continual influx of new data efficiently. Both algorithms employ novel data structures that facilitate 
the removal of outdated data panes, the integration of new panes, and the re-utilization of the existing panes within the sliding 
window, enabling FWUP mining through a single scan of the data. The FWUDS-CT algorithm employs a Circular Tidset data structure, 
an enhanced version of the tidset structure, that enables the rotation of transaction identifier values to avoid large numbers processing 
when data streams become very large. The FWUDS-DWT algorithm compresses data into a tree-based structure called DSWUN-tree, 
which enables quick data updates and generates a WUNList for patterns, thus optimizing the mining process. We have performed a 
series of experiments to assess these two algorithms across various datasets. The results indicate that the FWUDS-DWT algorithm 
significantly outperforms other algorithms in terms of runtime and scalability, though this efficiency comes at the cost of a bit higher 
memory consumption. Moving forward, we plan to extend our research to include mining frequent weighted utility closed patterns and 
top-k FWUPs in data streams. Additionally, we aim to explore the use of parallel programming architectures to further improve the 
performance of the algorithms and scalability for processing large-scale data. Another critical research direction involves adapting the 
proposed algorithms to real-time environments with fluctuating data inflow rates. While the current pane-based sliding window model
